# Referee report: *Early readers know letter shape without knowing letter names*

Children often chose the upright form of a Kannada letter they did not name, and children who made more accurate orientation choices tended to find upright letters faster than inverted ones. For the stronger interpretation to hold, the choice must reflect knowledge of that particular letter rather than a rule about how Kannada print usually looks, and the search advantage must not be explained by the letters children could name.

**Soundness: MAJOR_REVISION.** The first result is worth publishing. Of 300 children recruited from 14 schools, 285 took the orientation task, averaging 88.6% correct; the mean on the naming task was 53.8%. More telling than the difference between those means is Figure 2B: on items a child did not name, the authors report 91.8% correct orientation choices. In the 217 children who passed the search criterion, upright searches averaged 2.87 seconds versus 3.16 seconds inverted, with slightly *higher* upright accuracy (92.2% versus 90.9%). The group advantage is convincing for these children. The two central interpretations, that the children knew individual letter shapes without knowing their names and that this knowledge uniquely conferred the advantage, are only partly supported. Reconciling the samples, testing what the existing item-level data can distinguish, and narrowing the claims would bring this to minor revision. No new cohort is necessary for that paper.

**Strengths.** Using the same 18 akshara for orientation and naming makes it possible to ask what a given child could do with a given item. The orientation task gave positive feedback regardless of choice, so children were not simply taught the answer on each trial. The search task interleaved upright and inverted displays, including both target–distractor assignments for each pair. Finally, the authors did not rely solely on splitting children into groups: in the 181-child adjusted analysis, the familiarity association remained after the stated correction for nine tests (partial *r* = .23, *p* = .0016). That conditional association, rather than a claim about its cause, deserves to be prominent.

### Requests that bear on the conclusions

1. **Say what an orientation judgment measures.** The overview defines familiarity as “the ability to identify the correct orientation of a letter.” A child might know that certain strokes tend to sit at the top without having encountered this particular character. Few successfully named letters do not rule that out: the child can learn the regularity from print they cannot name. The correlation across items with and without the *tale kattu* is useful, but a correlation does not give the accuracy on letters without it. Report that accuracy against 50% with an interval. In the title, abstract and discussion, distinguish sensitivity to orientation from memory for individual forms. No new stimuli are needed if the interpretation leaves room for both mechanisms.

2. **Distinguish not naming from not knowing.** In Experiment 2, “each Kannada letter was shown once”; the child had to produce its name, whereas orientation was a two-choice pointing judgment repeated four times. A naming miss could reflect retrieval, reluctance to speak or lack of knowledge. And one unnamed letter gives a much less precise familiarity estimate than fifteen. For Figure 2B, report the numbers of contributing children and unnamed items per child, the treatment of empty sets, and how wrong names, “don't know” and silence were counted. Give an interval allowing for children and items, and show the result without silent trials. If it holds, “children chose the upright form of letters they did not name” captures a real dissociation without equating the two tasks' difficulty.

3. **Test the search claim on letters the child actually did not name.** The search used the “first 6 of the 18 in Experiment 1.” A child who named precisely those six could fall in the low-recognition group yet name every search stimulus. Estimate the upright contrast for pairs where the child named neither, one or both members; show how many children and trials support each estimate and give intervals. This is the most useful analysis of the existing data. All children also saw the six search letters *before* the familiarity task, equally often in both orientations. Does familiarity on the other 12 predict search? If the item-level comparison is too sparse, or the shared-item check cannot be made, say so and keep the result at the level of children's aggregate scores.

4. **Identify who contributes to each result.** Both 18-item tasks list 285 of the same 300 participants. At least 285 + 285 − 300 = 270 must therefore have taken both, yet Figure 2A describes “208 children who participated in both tasks.” A further search-eligibility filter could reconcile these counts. The abstract's statement that each child performed all four tasks also obscures the 59 who could not name any digits. Give the numbers and reasons at each step through Figure 2 and the 181-child adjusted model, including the test and cutoff for above-chance performance. Compare included and excluded children on available scores. Quantify, rather than just describe, the sensitivity of the familiarity association to the search cutoff and to inclusion of RAN in the model; the latter need not exclude non-digit-namers when that covariate is omitted. Report uncertainty and acknowledge when low-accuracy RTs are not interpretable.

5. **Separate a significant coefficient from a difference between coefficients.** In Figure 2, the two smaller, low-familiarity groups trend in the same direction as the larger groups. In Figure 3, regression on the same covariates as partial correlation is another expression of the conditional association, not independent confirmation of it. Give comparable standardized familiarity and naming effects with intervals. If one is claimed to be stronger, test their difference directly. Likewise, the kindergarten partial *r* of .33 and Grade 1 partial *r* of .06 do not by themselves show moderation, especially given Grade 1's ceiling. Give interaction estimates and intervals for claims about grade or medium, or describe the subgroup results without claiming a difference or invariance. Distinguish the corrected primary tests from exploratory comparisons. In interpreting the contrast, discuss the reliability of children's scores: reproducible **rankings of letters** across halves of the sample do not establish reliable **rankings of children**.

6. **Separate the school context from the source of learning.** Medium varies between eight Kannada-medium and six English-medium schools; Figure 3's bootstrap resamples children. Give a school-aware estimate and uncertainty for the main association, retaining age and grade, or state what cannot be separated with these schools. Explain what is known about Kannada instruction in each grade and medium, language of testing, and children's home-script exposure. The authors already acknowledge that exposure at home and later literacy were not measured. That qualification belongs beside “which confers on them a visual processing advantage” in the abstract. Neither a child's age nor a kindergarten label rules out prior teaching or writing practice. The measured result is an association in this Kannada sample, not its developmental order, causal source or effect on later reading.

### Methods and minor comments

7. **Make the RT analysis reproducible.** Specify which responses, timeouts and repeats entered each child's mean; describe the “baseline motor task” and its timing and scoring. Give the actual model formula and coefficient units: the results say both nine and eight predictors. A difference in seconds removes a constant delay but can still scale with a child's overall search speed. Show the familiarity effect with an interval on a log or proportional RT scale, or acknowledge that limitation. Reconcile the two accounts of RAN timing: Participants puts it in sessions 3 or 4, before sessions 9–10; Procedure puts it after search.

8. **Correct the RAN conclusion and caption.** Figure 4's text reports partial associations for *both* letter naming (*r* = .46) and medium (*r* = −.38), then says RAN is “uniquely associated with letter recognition accuracy.” Name both. Verify that Figure 4B actually plots RAN and correct the caption, which identifies the outcome as the search advantage.

9. **Keep the full-set letter result primary.** The text reports “no correlation across all letters” before removing ಣ. Its similarity to the inverted form is a plausible reason for its behavior, and precisely the kind of variation the orientation task measures. Label the 17-letter result exploratory rather than generalizing it to all letters, or show the full-set and leave-one-letter-out estimates with intervals.

10. **Clarify the independent-group tests.** “Unpaired signed-rank test” does not identify an unambiguous procedure. If this was a rank-sum test, correct the name; if paired signed ranks were used for independent children, recompute those comparisons. This and point 9 are should-fix items, not reasons for the editorial decision.

**Data and code.** The authors state that all data and code required to reproduce the results are available at https://osf.io/v79km/. I did not access the repository in this manuscript-only review, so I cannot assess completeness or runnability. The sample flow and RT definitions in points 4 and 7 are necessary to interpret the paper even if the code supplies them.

**Significance and venue fit.** This is a worthwhile contribution to developmental reading and visual cognition. As the manuscript describes them, Levy et al. (2006) addressed discrimination of print from other forms, Nag and Snowling (2012) related akshara knowledge to reading, and Agrawal et al. (2022) studied upright bigram search in older children. The useful step here is to place an orientation choice, a spoken name and search performance side by side near school entry. I have not independently verified novelty. The descriptive advance fits a specialist journal; a broader claim about how print knowledge develops would require distinguishing learned script regularities from memory for particular letters.

**Path to a stronger paper, in priority order.** First, lead with the result on unnamed items and give its denominator and uncertainty (points 1, 2 and 4). Second, ask whether the upright advantage persists when neither search letter was named, and whether it survives the school-aware analysis (points 3 and 6). Third, *only if pursuing the stronger mechanism*, test whether children choose the conventional orientation just as readily for never-seen, Kannada-like forms matched for visual cues. A comparable receptive name-choice task could separately distinguish failure of spoken retrieval from failure to associate the sound with the letter. Those would be valuable follow-ups, not conditions for publishing an accurately framed result.
